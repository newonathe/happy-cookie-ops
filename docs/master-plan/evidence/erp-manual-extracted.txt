Executive Summary
A web-based ERP startup is not merely a software-development company. It is a coordinated system for discovering business 
problems, converting them into reusable product capabilities, implementing those capabilities safely inside customer 
organizations, and operating the resulting service over years. The research base supplied for this report correctly identified the 
central structural idea: the startup should run two linked lifecycles rather than one undifferentiated project stream - a 
product/platform lifecycle and a customer implementation lifecycle. That distinction is preserved and expanded throughout 
this manual.
Two linked lifecycles: product/platform and customer implementation
The strongest operating model is therefore:
1. A product company that owns one coherent platform, common domain models, reusable modules, architecture 
standards, security controls, deployment tooling, product analytics, and a governed roadmap.
2. An implementation organization that qualifies customers, discovers processes, maps requirements, performs fit-to-
standard and fit-gap analysis, configures and extends the platform, migrates data, integrates surrounding systems, 
validates the target operating model, manages cutover, and supports adoption.
3. A service operation that treats go-live as the beginning of the production lifecycle, not the end of development. It 
owns monitoring, incident response, vulnerability management, backups, release management, support, service 
reviews, and continuous improvement.
The most important strategic recommendation is to begin with a vertical ERP wedge rather than attempting to build a complete 
horizontal ERP suite at once. Pick a customer segment in which you understand a painful end-to-end value stream, such as 
distribution order-to-cash and inventory, project-based professional services, specialty manufacturing, field operations, or a 
similarly coherent vertical. Build the smallest domain spine that solves that value stream extremely well, then widen the product 
through validated customer learning.
The operating rule should be:
Sell a measurable business outcome; discover the actual process; adopt or configure standard behavior first; 
extend only where justified; preserve one core product; automate quality, security, and operations early; and 
feed every implementation lesson back into product knowledge.

1.2 Non-negotiable design principles
Principle A - Process before feature
ERP is an operating system for business processes. A feature list is an implementation artifact, not the starting point. The 
provider should understand the trigger, actors, decisions, data, exceptions, controls, handoffs, outputs, and KPIs of the process 
before deciding what software behavior is required.
Principle B - One product, many implementations
Customer implementations may differ, but the startup should preserve one versioned product core. Differences should be 
expressed through configuration, extension points, integrations, customer data, permissions, templates, and isolated custom 
extensions rather than forks.
Principle C - Configuration before customization
Fit-to-standard analysis exists to challenge the assumption that a legacy process must be reproduced. If standard behavior can 
meet the business objective, adopting it reduces design, development, regression, training, and upgrade burden. [S2]
Principle D - Traceability from outcome to operation
Every important production behavior should be traceable from business objective through requirement, design, test, training, 
and operational measurement.
Requirements and delivery traceability chain
Principle E - Security, privacy, quality, and operability are product requirements
They are not final-week checklists. Authorization, tenant isolation, auditability, observability, recovery, data lifecycle, supply-
chain security, and performance must be designed and tested along with features.
Principle F - Go-live is a lifecycle transition
The implementation moves from project delivery into production ownership. Hypercare, support, SLO management, 
vulnerability remediation, backup verification, capacity management, and release governance are part of the ERP product.
Principle G - Measurable business value is the ultimate acceptance test
The provider should know the customer’s baseline and target: order cycle time, inventory accuracy, days to close, procurement 
turnaround, manual re-entry, approval latency, collection performance, utilization, or another vertical KPI. A feature delivered 
without a business outcome is not automatically value delivered.

2.2 Define a vertical wedge
Do not define the target as “SMEs that need ERP.” Define it as a recognizable operating context. A useful ICP definition 
includes:
• industry or sub-industry;
• employee and transaction scale;
• number of entities, branches, warehouses, or operating sites;
• dominant value stream;
• incumbent systems and spreadsheet dependence;
• trigger event that makes change urgent;
• regulatory or reporting complexity;
• target buyer and economic decision maker;
• implementation readiness.
Good vertical wedges have three properties:
1. Pain is frequent and measurable. The problem occurs every day or every accounting period.
2. The process repeats across customers. Reusability is high enough to build a product.
3. The wedge expands naturally. Solving one value stream creates credible adjacent modules.
Example expansion:
Distributor wedge
  -> customer/product master
  -> quotations and sales orders
  -> inventory and warehouse
  -> purchasing
  -> invoicing and receivables
  -> payables
  -> GL and reporting
  -> e-commerce / EDI / bank integrations
  -> planning and analytics

4.3 Qualification method
BANT - Budget, Authority, Need, Timeline - is a useful minimum structure, but complex ERP deals usually need more than 
BANT because multiple functions, process owners, IT/security reviewers, procurement, finance, and an economic buyer can all 
participate. Use a hybrid qualification record with concepts from complex B2B methods such as MEDDIC/MEDDICC where 
appropriate: measurable outcomes, economic buyer, decision criteria, decision process, pain, champion, and 
competition/alternatives.
Mandatory gates before expensive solution work:
• a material business problem exists;
• an internal sponsor/champion exists;
• there is a plausible path to an economic decision maker;
• the target use case fits the product strategy;
• a credible timing trigger exists;
• customer resources for discovery, data, UAT, and decisions are available;
• budget is known or at least plausible relative to scope.
Optional 0-3 scoring can help prioritization, but never let a high total score compensate for the absence of a real business 
problem or sponsor.

5. Discovery and Business Process Engineering
5.1 Discovery is not a requirements shopping list
The wrong question is: “What features do you want?”
The better question is: “Walk us through the business event from beginning to end, including the exceptions.”
For each end-to-end process, capture:
Actor -> Trigger -> Input -> Decision/rule -> Action -> System -> Data changed -> Approval -> Exception -> Output -> 
Control -> KPI.

5.4 AS-IS vs TO-BE discipline
First document the actual current process. Mark:
• duplicate entry;
• waiting time;
• manual reconciliation;
• weak ownership;
• missing approval evidence;
• work performed only because of old software;
• undocumented exceptions;
• data quality failure;
• compliance/control weakness;
• bottlenecks and handoff latency.
Then design the TO-BE process around the target outcome. Do not automate a broken process merely because it is familiar.

6.3 Make NFRs measurable
Bad: “The system must be fast.”
Better:

are explicitly classified as asynchronous or long-running.

modify, export, infer, or cause processing of Tenant B business data through
UI, APIs, search, caches, object storage, queues, background jobs, reports,
or administrative interfaces unless explicitly operating under a governed

11. Architecture Strategy for a Startup ERP
11.1 Recommended default: modular monolith plus asynchronous workers
The uploaded research recommended a modular monolith rather than early microservices. That remains a sound default, not a 
universal rule. The reason is operational economics: a small team usually benefits from one deployable application, 
straightforward local transactions, easier debugging, fewer network failure modes, and lower platform overhead while still 
enforcing module boundaries.
Extract services when a domain has a real reason for independent ownership or scaling, for example:
• integration processing with very different scaling;
• heavy reporting/analytics;
• document generation;
• AI/optimization services;
• independently regulated domain;
• separate availability/security boundary;
• clear bounded context owned by an autonomous team.
Do not begin with dozens of services merely to appear enterprise-grade.

12. ERP Domain Engineering: Correctness Before CRUD
ERP software is not simply a set of forms over database tables. It encodes business invariants. A technically elegant 
application can still be dangerous if financial, inventory, approval, or audit semantics are wrong.
12.1 Financial domain invariants
Monetary representation
Use fixed-precision decimal semantics appropriate to currency and calculation rules. Define:
• transaction currency;
• base/functional currency;
• exchange-rate source and effective date;
• precision by currency and quantity;
• line-level vs document-level rounding;
• tax rounding;
• realized/unrealized FX treatment where relevant.
Do not use binary floating point as the authoritative representation of posted money.
Double-entry accounting
For every posted journal:
Sum(debits) = Sum(credits)
Enforce this invariant in domain logic and tests. A journal should have a header plus lines, posting date, legal entity/ledger, 
currency context, source document, status, dimensions, audit information, and a posting identifier.
Posted transactions are records, not editable drafts
Once a financial transaction is posted, correction should normally occur through reversal, credit/debit note, adjustment, or 
another governed accounting action. Avoid silently editing or deleting posted historical values.

Period control
Support open/closed fiscal periods and role-controlled reopening if the market requires it. Prevent ordinary posting into a closed 
period.
Subledger to GL reconciliation
AR, AP, inventory, fixed assets and other subledgers should reconcile to control accounts. Build reconciliation as a first-class 
process, not a reporting afterthought.
Document numbering
Define numbering scopes and uniqueness: legal entity, branch, document class, fiscal year, statutory sequence, or customer 
requirement. Never generate important business numbers solely from a client-side counter.
Segregation of duties
Examples of conflicting capabilities:
• create vendor + approve vendor + pay vendor;
• create PO + receive + approve invoice + release payment;
• create journal + approve/post journal;
• change bank details + release payment;
• alter user permissions + approve sensitive transactions.
Your authorization model should be capable of policy checks beyond a flat role list.

14.1 Treat data ownership as governance

• authoritative source;
• business owner/steward;
• unique/business keys;
• lifecycle states;
• duplicate policy;

Typical master data includes organizations, legal entities, branches, customers, vendors, items, UOMs, warehouses, accounts,

14.2 Relational truth first
ERP core data is highly relational and transactional. Use normalized relational models for authoritative commercial, inventory 
and accounting records. JSON can be appropriate for controlled metadata, connector payloads, form configuration or custom-
field envelopes, but not as a substitute for modeling business invariants.
14.3 Deletion policy
Do not use blanket soft-delete for everything. Classify records:
• mutable master data that can be inactivated;
• drafts that may be safely deleted;
• posted transactions that must be retained/reversed;
• personal data subject to retention/deletion rules;
• derived/cache data that can be regenerated.

15.3 Reliable side effects: outbox/inbox pattern
When a committed ERP transaction must reliably create an asynchronous external event, avoid a distributed transaction across 
your database and message broker. A common approach is:
1. commit the business transaction and an outbound-event record atomically;
2. worker publishes the event;
3. mark publication outcome;
4. consumer uses a message/event identifier to tolerate duplicates;
5. reconciliation detects missing/divergent records.
Exactly-once behavior across independent external systems is frequently unrealistic. Design for at-least-once delivery + 
idempotent handling + reconciliation unless a stronger guarantee is genuinely supported end to end.

• RTO - maximum tolerable recovery duration.

33.1 Computerized accounting systems and books
The Philippine BIR continues to maintain registration processes for Computerized Accounting Systems (CAS),

record sales, purchases, AR, AP, inventory, payroll, subsidiary ledgers and reports as within the relevant computerized-

BIR Revenue Regulation No. 11-2025 implements statutory provisions concerning electronic invoices and electronic sales

• controller/processor role analysis;

• breach/incident process;

37. Founder-Level Operating Rules

• larger identity/SoD models;
• much higher transaction and batch volume;
• shared-service centers;
• global versus local master-data ownership;
• multiple rollout waves;
• integration portfolios rather than individual interfaces;
• formal architecture, risk, audit and change governance.

43.9 Critical invariants and tests
• no two concurrent reservations may consume the same unavailable unit;
• on-hand, reserved, available and in-transit quantities reconcile by location/status;

OSS/BSS Integration
66.1 Product boundary - ERP is not the whole telco stack
A telecom has enterprise ERP for finance, procurement, assets/projects and HCM, but revenue/customer/network operations 
often live in BSS/OSS. TM Forum’s Open Digital Architecture provides current business, information, implementation and API 
architecture for CSPs; TM Forum Open APIs are designed for interoperable lifecycle management across multi-partner digital 
services. [S61]
Oracle Communications BRM is an example of a specialized BSS revenue platform that configures offers/accounts, charges 
usage, bills, takes payment and manages customer revenue - functions far beyond ordinary ERP AR. [S62]
Therefore a startup must choose:
• internal telco ERP;
• OSS/BSS integration platform;
• specialized billing/revenue product;
• field/network asset product;
rather than claiming to build “telecom ERP” as one module.

66.5 Integration architecture
TM Forum’s current Open API program includes REST, event-driven and domain-specific APIs plus a shared data model, and

66.6 Critical non-functional requirements
Telecom can impose much higher event/billing scale than ordinary ERP:
• high-volume usage ingestion;
• rating/billing batch windows;
• real-time charging latency where applicable;
• catalog/version compatibility;
• event replay/idempotency;
• revenue reconciliation;
• partner settlement;
• very high availability.
A small ERP startup should usually enter through enterprise ERP integration or a narrow BSS capability, not attempt a 
carrier-grade converged charging platform as an MVP.
67. Banking, Insurance and Financial-Services Internal ERP - 
Boundary Playbook
Financial institutions use ERP for enterprise finance, procurement, HCM, assets, projects and expense management. Core 
banking, trading, policy administration, claims, payment switching and risk engines are not ordinary ERP modules. 
Oracle’s current banking documentation, for example, describes a separate core-banking platform for account lifecycle, 
transaction control, deposits, pricing and channel integration, while Oracle’s insurance suite separately handles policy 
administration and claims adjudication. These are useful boundary examples: they show why an ERP provider should integrate 
to specialized financial-services cores rather than silently rebuilding them as ERP submodules. [S68] This distinction is 
strategically important because replacing a bank’s core ledger/transaction platform requires a completely different regulatory 
and technical program.

68. Deployment and Tenancy Overlays
Industry playbooks change what the ERP does. Deployment overlays change how it is delivered, isolated, upgraded and

ERP deployment and tenancy overlays
68.1 Pooled multi-tenant SaaS
Multiple tenants share application and potentially database resources. Tenant isolation is an explicit security property separate

not enforced. [S66]

• tenant control plane/onboarding;
• tenant-scoped identity and authorization;
• tenant-aware database/query rules;
• tenant-aware cache keys, queues, object paths and search indexes;
• per-tenant config/feature entitlement;
• metering/billing;
• tenant-aware telemetry without sensitive leakage;
• isolation automated tests;
• noisy-neighbor and quota controls;

68.5 Hybrid ERP
Hybrid means material components remain in different locations, such as cloud ERP with local MES/machines, local fiscal

• certificate/credential lifecycle;

• reconciliation after connectivity outage;
• version compatibility across cloud and edge.

71.1 Recommended startup sequence
For most new ERP providers, the lower-risk route is:
1. Choose one vertical and one painful end-to-end flow.
2. Build the shared platform core.
3. Build only the functional domains needed by that flow.
4. Deliver to design partners using the universal methodology.
5. Measure repeated gaps and convert them into configuration/product IP.
6. Standardize the vertical playbook, migration templates and tests.
7. Add adjacent processes within the same vertical.
8. Only then enter an adjacent vertical that can reuse most of the domain spine.

Wholesale distribution

74.9 Research status and refresh rule
This edition was cross-checked against public sources available as of 7 September 2026. It deliberately distinguishes:
• normative/mandatory evidence - law, regulator or binding standard when applicable;
• recognized standards/frameworks - accounting, industry, interoperability and lifecycle references;
• mature implementation/vendor evidence - useful for process patterns, interfaces and product boundaries but not automatically 
prescriptive;
• startup operating judgment - recommended sequencing, scoring, staffing, architecture and commercial heuristics that must be 
adapted to the target vertical and customer.
Versions, tax/e-invoicing rules, security standards, cloud services, healthcare/education/telecom interoperability specifications and sector 
regulation change. The provider should therefore maintain a quarterly evidence refresh, plus a mandatory pre-contract/pre-release legal-and-
regulatory review for any regulated vertical or new jurisdiction.
